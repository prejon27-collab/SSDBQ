A quick orientation

The Constitution creates a national government, divides its powers, and sets limits on what government may do.

How it is organized

The Preamble states the goals. Articles I–III establish Congress, the presidency, and the federal courts. Articles IV–VII address relations among states, amendment, national law, and ratification. Amendments add or change constitutional rules.

Limited government

Federal officials have only the powers granted by the Constitution, along with powers reasonably necessary to carry them out. The Constitution also directly prohibits certain government actions.

Articles I and VI; Tenth Amendment

Separation of powers

Legislative, executive, and judicial authority is assigned to different institutions. Each branch has its own responsibilities and tools to check the others.

Articles I, II, and III

Popular sovereignty

The Constitution begins, “We the People.” Public authority is grounded in the people, exercised through the constitutional system of elections and representative government.

Preamble; Article I; Amendments XV, XIX, XXIV, and XXVI
Study tip: Start with the branch that holds a power, then ask what limits or checks that power. For example, Congress passes bills, the President may veto them, and Congress may override a veto.

The seven articles

Open an article to review its sections and the main constitutional rules they establish.

The 27 amendments

The first ten are the Bill of Rights. Later amendments address citizenship, voting, government structure, and other subjects.

Three branches, shared power

Each branch has distinct constitutional duties. Their overlapping checks help prevent power from gathering in one place.

Article I

Congress

The legislative branch makes federal laws. Congress is bicameral: the House of Representatives and the Senate must generally agree on the same bill.

Core powers

  • Levy taxes, borrow money, and spend for the common defense and general welfare.
  • Regulate interstate and foreign commerce; establish naturalization and bankruptcy rules.
  • Declare war, raise and support armed forces, and make laws necessary and proper to carry out enumerated powers.
  • The Senate confirms many high-level appointments and ratifies treaties; the House impeaches, and the Senate tries impeachments.
Article II

The President

The executive branch carries out federal law. The President is commander in chief and leads the executive departments, subject to constitutional and statutory limits.

Core powers and duties

  • Sign or veto bills; Congress can override a veto with two-thirds votes in both chambers.
  • Make treaties with the Senate’s advice and consent; nominate ambassadors, judges, and other officers with Senate confirmation where required.
  • Appoint officers, grant federal reprieves and pardons except in impeachment cases, and report to Congress on the state of the Union.
  • “Take Care” that the laws are faithfully executed. The President does not make legislation alone.
Article III

The federal courts

The judicial branch interprets and applies federal law in cases properly before it. The Constitution establishes the Supreme Court and allows Congress to create lower federal courts.

Core powers and limits

  • Federal judicial power covers cases arising under the Constitution, federal laws, and treaties, along with specified other categories.
  • Courts decide concrete cases and controversies; they do not issue advisory opinions.
  • Judges hold office during good behavior, and their compensation cannot be reduced while they remain in office.
  • Judicial review—the power to assess the constitutionality of government action—was established in Marbury v. Madison (1803), not expressly spelled out in the text.
Checks and balances: Congress can impeach federal officers; the President nominates judges subject to Senate confirmation; courts resolve cases about constitutional and legal disputes; and Congress controls appropriations and may override presidential vetoes.

Key constitutional ideas

Quick, grounded explanations of terms that often come up when studying the Constitution.

Habeas corpus

A legal procedure requiring the government to justify a person’s detention before a court. The Constitution says the privilege of the writ may not be suspended unless rebellion or invasion makes public safety require it.

Article I, Section 9, Clause 2

Due process

Government must follow fair legal procedures before depriving a person of life, liberty, or property. The Fifth Amendment applies this guarantee to the federal government; the Fourteenth applies it to the states.

Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments

Equal protection

States must provide equal protection of the laws to people within their jurisdiction. The principle is in the Fourteenth Amendment and is central to constitutional equality cases.

Fourteenth Amendment, Section 1

Supremacy Clause

The Constitution, valid federal laws made under it, and treaties are the supreme law of the land. State judges must follow them when state law conflicts, subject to the Constitution’s limits on federal power.

Article VI, Clause 2

Necessary and Proper Clause

Congress may make laws necessary and proper for carrying out its enumerated powers and other powers vested in the federal government. It supports implied powers; it is not a standalone unlimited power.

Article I, Section 8, Clause 18

Commerce Clause

Congress may regulate commerce with foreign nations, among the states, and with Native American tribes. The boundaries of this power have been shaped by constitutional text and Supreme Court decisions.

Article I, Section 8, Clause 3

Bill of attainder

A law that singles out a person or group for punishment without a judicial trial. The Constitution prohibits both Congress and the states from passing bills of attainder.

Article I, Sections 9 and 10

Ex post facto law

A law that retroactively makes conduct criminal or increases punishment for conduct already committed. The Constitution prohibits these laws at both the federal and state levels.

Article I, Sections 9 and 10

Impeachment

The House has the sole power to impeach federal officers. The Senate has the sole power to try impeachments; conviction requires two-thirds of senators present. Conviction can remove an officer and disqualify them from future federal office, but does not prevent separate criminal proceedings.

Article I, Sections 2–3; Article II, Section 4

Federalism

Power is divided between the national government and the states. The Tenth Amendment reserves powers not delegated to the United States, nor prohibited to the states, to the states or the people.

Article IV; Tenth Amendment
